The Dialogue between “Om” and “Xiang”

In my hometown of Xiangcheng, household stoves have a distinctive local style. People paint or sculpt a range of images connected with Tibetan Buddhism on the stove walls, and the decorated stove is honoured as the Stove God. Most stoves share a similar form, and the most striking feature is the cat found in almost every household. The cat is not a common motif in Tibetan Buddhist iconography, so why does it appear on these stoves and receive such respect? Why is it so similar in form and function to the jewel-spitting mongoose? Starting with these questions, I used the stove as a way to examine how images and material forms carry social memory and take part in shaping local and ethnic identity in Xiangcheng. This project later became my master’s thesis.

Embodied Knowledge and Cultural Transmission in Traditional Puppetry

Over three months, we studied theatre theory, puppet making, and rod-puppet performance. We also took part in training and performances as members of a local troupe. Making and operating puppets showed me how craft knowledge is passed on through demonstration, bodily practice, and coordination on stage. This experience led me to consider how embodied knowledge continues through performance, and how traditional puppetry helps preserve local memory, shape a shared sense of belonging, and maintain community ties.

Ecologies of Heritage: Cultural Revitalization in Rural China

On Bashao Island in Dalian, Liaoning, I conducted fieldwork on local customs and intangible cultural heritage, with particular attention to their connections with the island’s ecology and community life. Drawing on other cases studied by my supervisor’s team, we identified several shared questions and proposed an approach that gives greater attention to local environments and community participation. The experience of Bashao Island also offered a useful point of comparison for cultural preservation in other rural areas. This was my first attempt to study folklore and intangible cultural heritage within their living settings. It showed me that cultural continuity is closely tied to local ecology, everyday life, and the choices of community members.

From Ritual Governance to Aesthetic Transcendence: A Semiotic Genealogy of “乐”

Throughout the history of Chinese, the character “乐” developed several readings and meanings. Yuè mainly refers to music, lè to joy and happiness, and yào to liking or appreciation. This project asks how a single character came to connect such different ideas and experiences over time. Through a historical study of the character and related texts, I explored how “乐” became part of ritual and political order, everyday emotion, and aesthetic thought. I also used a three-dimensional diagram to show the historical links among these meanings. This was my first attempt to use one character to examine the connections among language, history, and everyday life.

Motifs of Change

At the time, the animal welfare organization I led at my university received an opportunity to carry out a project. Because I come from a Tibetan region, we began with our shared interest in animals and turned our attention to the animal motifs on Tibetan rugs. We considered how animals were imagined and depicted, and why certain motifs remained over time. As the research progressed, we found that rug patterns, colours, and methods of production changed with the market and the wider economic and policy environment. Some animal motifs remained relatively stable. We also studied how the rugs were produced, processed, and sold, looking at how images and material forms take shape through craft work, local experience, and market demand. This was my first attempt to connect animal images, material objects, and economic processes. It also led me to consider how visual forms and material objects enter everyday life.

Grassroots Governance and Community Dynamics in Urban China

My undergraduate studies taught me that grassroots governance is not simply a top-down process led by local governments and grassroots Party organizations. Community residents also play an important role. Across Chengdu, communities in different urban districts vary greatly in both the forms and results of resident participation. Some award-winning communities have developed effective ways to involve residents, while others have seen active participation but limited results. To understand how effective participation takes shape, we studied several communities where local governance had worked well. Through interviews conducted in these communities and questionnaire surveys, we summarized their experience and proposed suggestions suited to different local conditions. This research also helped me see that residents’ everyday lives cannot be separated from politics at the grassroots level. Community governance takes shape through everyday needs, neighborhood relations, and participation in local public affairs. The project later became the basis of my bachelor’s thesis.

Visual Culture and Material Memory in Chinese Revolutionary Heritage Sites

Compared with well-known revolutionary heritage sites such as Jinggangshan, some former revolutionary base areas in Sichuan have received less public attention. Yet these places are equally important in historical and political terms, and their local heritage has had fewer opportunities to be presented and developed. This led us to conduct fieldwork in Guangyuan, Luzhou, and other places. Through comparative research and extensive interviews, we explored how local governments and residents preserve, present, and pass on their own revolutionary history and culture. We also proposed several practical suggestions for its future development. This project was the first time I became aware that material culture is not simply a passive carrier of history. It also shapes how people understand the past and experience local life. This realization led me to turn my attention to visual and material culture.
